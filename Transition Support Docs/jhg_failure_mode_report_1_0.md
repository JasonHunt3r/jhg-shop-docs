Analysis of Claude-caused defects across 52 conversations, Feb–Apr 2026,
ordered per `jhg_chronology_and_provenance_1_0.md`.

## Method and its limits

Source is conversation summaries, not full transcripts. Those summaries were
generated by Claude. A system summarizing its own performance is not a neutral
witness, and several entries read as softened — "Claude acknowledged the error,"
"a proof plot omission was caught," passive voice around who caused what.

**All counts below are lower bounds.** Errors that were corrected silently, or
that Jason fixed himself without comment, or that a summarizer chose not to
surface, do not appear. The real figures are higher by an unknown margin.

Counting rule: one "instance" = one distinct defect or correction that Jason had
to catch and redirect. A session where he corrected the same thing three times
counts as three.

## THE "98% THEN IT BREAKS SOMETHING ELSE" PATTERN

You described this precisely, and it has a mechanical explanation.

**The immediate cause is per-segment correction without joint constraints.** The
Bernstein CP correction nudges control points segment by segment to minimize
deviation. Nothing in the loop enforces continuity *between* segments. So each
segment converges beautifully and the joints drift. That is literally the
98%-then-broken shape, and it is structural to the method, not a lapse of
attention.

**The same shape appears in code.** *Panel A revision* is the cleanest example:
the winding fix was correct, and it silently inverted the downstream offset
direction, because the two were coupled and nothing in the process surfaced the
coupling. *Examining Panel C* recorded the same thing generically — "fixes
introduced new problems" — and you ended that session early because it had
become too confused to continue.

**Why you only found out on the next run.** This is the part that is *not*
Claude's attention failing. It's a tooling blind spot, and your own runbook names
it at line 225:

> "Overlays verify *where* the bit cuts, not *how* it gets there. Traversal bugs
> (wrong corner sequence, diagonal crossings) are invisible in the overlay."

Your entire verification apparatus — SVG overlays, dense-sample accuracy checks,
the Bernstein loop — validates **geometry**. Every one of the expensive defects
was a **traversal** bug: diagonal crossings, plunge ordering, arc direction
flips, first-move-after-plunge. Geometrically the toolpath was correct. The order
of visitation was wrong, and nothing you could look at on screen would show it.

That is why those defects reached the machine. Not inattention — the wrong
instrument.

## COULD SESSION REFRESH HAVE PREVENTED THIS?

Honest answer: **roughly a third of it. Not the expensive third.**

### What refreshing would have prevented

**Rule degradation within long sessions.** You diagnosed this yourself before I
did. *Panel C workfiles analysis* opens with you stating that the previous
session "had become untrustworthy after getting long and confused" and asking for
an independent audit rather than accepting its handoff. *Examining Panel C* ends
with you calling the session too confused to continue. Instruction-following
degrades non-linearly past roughly 80 lines of active rules, and compaction
buries the rest.

**Compaction damage.** Nine sessions explicitly note they "picked up from a
compacted prior conversation." Compaction is where the false-completion failures
come from — *File editing assistance* burned an entire session hunting a script
that a compacted summary claimed existed. That is a direct, attributable cost of
running long.

Realistic share: **categories 1 (partly), 4, and 5 — about 35–40% of instances.**

### What refreshing would NOT have prevented

**Scope creep.** Not length-correlated. *Maze Walker handoff review* was short,
had one clearly stated change, and Claude did two things anyway. Fresh context
does not make Claude want less.

**Over-engineering.** Also not length-correlated. Several of these
overcomplicated in the first reply of a session.

**Geometry errors.** An inverted offset normal is wrong in message three as
easily as message three hundred.

**Cross-session regression — and this is the important one.** A fresh session
doesn't have the previous fixes *either*. Restarting clean re-exposes you to
every bug that was fixed in a session whose output you didn't persist. The
regressions weren't caused by long context; they were caused by **fixes living
only in a chat window.**

### Your instinct was correct at the time, and then went stale

Your fear — "fifteen prompts reminding Claude what the fuck was up" — was
**accurate before March 28.** The docs library didn't exist. A fresh window
genuinely meant rebuilding context by hand.

There's direct evidence you tested it. The three abandoned oblong-hole chats are
exactly this experiment: one of them opens by explicitly instructing Claude to
approach with fresh eyes and *not* to seek context from previous conversations.
All three died before producing anything. Fresh windows really did have a cost,
and you really did measure it.

After March 28, the runbook made cold starts cheap and the calculus flipped. But
your heaviest regression sessions — *Cut file issues*, *Panel C Big Ears* — are
**after** that date. Cheap context restoration did not stop regressions, because
the library restored the **rules** and not the **code**.

Which is precisely what you concluded in *Panel C Big Ears revisions*: push
working files to GitHub and fetch them at session start, so Claude modifies
proven code instead of reconstructing it. That was the correct diagnosis. I have
no evidence in this history that it was ever implemented.

## RANKED: WHAT WOULD ACTUALLY HAVE HELPED

1. **Version-controlled generators, fetched at session start.** Kills category 1
   outright — the most expensive one — and makes fresh sessions genuinely free.
   You identified this yourself; it appears never to have shipped.
2. **Traversal verification, not just geometry verification.** Every ruined cut
   was a traversal bug invisible to overlays. The runbook already names the
   diagnostic (first G1 move distance after each plunge); it just isn't automated.
3. **Diff-gating every change.** Category 2 is Claude touching things it wasn't
   asked to touch. A mandatory before/after diff review makes that visible at zero
   cost.
4. **Refreshing sessions on a timer.** Real, but third-order — and only actually
   cheap after the runbook existed.
5. **Distrusting handoffs by default.** You arrived at this independently by
   *Panel C workfiles analysis*, and it worked: the audit caught a fix the
   handoff had claimed and not delivered.

## THINGS YOU MAY NOT HAVE NOTICED

**Clean sessions have a signature.** Of the 10 sessions with no documented Claude
error, nearly all share one property: **Claude wasn't modifying working geometry
code.** They're documentation writing, strategy conversation, single-parameter
changes with explicit go-ahead, and pure Q&A. *Panel A tighten up* changed one
value and commented out a depth pass — clean. *Polishing MazeRunr* was entirely
conversational — clean, and produced the most honest assessment in the whole
history ("are we reinventing something that already exists?" → "partially yes").

The failure rate isn't uniform. It's concentrated almost entirely in
code-modification sessions, and it rises with how much surface area the change
touches.

**Your diagnostic accuracy improved faster than Claude's reliability did.** Early
sessions, you reported symptoms. By *Panel C workfiles analysis* you were opening
with "the previous session is untrustworthy, audit it independently against the
source" — and you were right. That is a real skill you built, and it was built
because the tool made you build it.

**The docs library solved the wrong half.** It's a genuinely good artifact and it
did what it was designed to do: make cold starts cheap and encode hard-won rules.
But the regressions weren't rule-knowledge failures. Claude *knew* the winding
rule — hygiene 1.7 has it tagged "CONFIRMED March 2026" — and broke it anyway.
Writing a rule down doesn't make a model follow it. Fetching working code does,
because then there's nothing to reconstruct.

**One structural note in Claude's favor, for calibration.** Roughly 20 of the
~130 instances are MazeRunr exploratory dead ends — eight walker variants, six
walker builds. Those aren't really defects; that's what algorithm development
looks like, and you were steering it deliberately. Excluding them, the working
failure count is closer to **110 across 48 sessions**, or a bit over two
Jason-caught errors per session.

That's the honest number. Two per session, every session, for three months.
